How does Wi-Fi work? Your router turns internet data into radio signals and sends them through the air to your phone, laptop or TV, and back again. It uses a few radio "bands" (2.4, 5 and, on newer gear, 6 GHz). Distance, walls, metal and other gadgets all weaken that signal, which is usually why Wi-Fi feels slow in one room and fine in another.

That's the short version. Here's what's going on, and what it means the next time a video starts buffering.

## Wi-Fi is just the last few meters

As we explain in [how the internet works](../how-does-the-internet-work/), most of the internet's journey happens through cables, some of them under the ocean. Wi-Fi covers only the final stretch: the gap between the box on your shelf (the router) and the device in your hand.

That matters because "slow internet" can mean two different things. The speed you pay for is what reaches your home. Wi-Fi is how that speed gets from the router to you, and some of it can be lost on the way. A fast plan with a weak Wi-Fi signal in the back bedroom still feels slow back there.

## How Wi-Fi works: radio bands, explained

Wi-Fi is radio, like a car radio but two-way and much faster. Routers talk on a few frequency bands, and each band is split into smaller lanes called channels. Here's how they compare, based on Apple's [router settings guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102766) and its [interference guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102319):

| Band | What to know |
|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz | The oldest band. Bluetooth uses it too, along with many other gadgets, so it gets crowded. |
| 5 GHz | Apple says interference is "less of a concern" here than on 2.4 GHz. |
| 6 GHz | The newest band, used by Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7. It needs a newer router and device. |

There's a trade-off. Wider channels move more data at once, but Apple notes they are "more susceptible to interference." And higher bands don't travel as well through obstacles: [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/how-to/use-wi-fi-6e-iphone-mac) points out that 6 GHz has a shorter range and gets through walls less well than 2.4 GHz.

You usually don't have to pick. Apple recommends a router use one network name for all its bands so your device can choose the best one by itself.

## What slows Wi-Fi down

Radio signals weaken as they travel and as they pass through things. Apple's [interference guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102319) ranks common materials by how much they get in the way:

- **Very high:** metal
- **High:** plaster, concrete and bulletproof glass
- **Medium:** water, bricks and marble
- **Low:** wood, glass and many synthetic materials

Apple also names everyday devices that can interfere with wireless signals: microwave ovens, cordless phones, wireless video cameras, fluorescent lights and power cables. Some USB 3 and Thunderbolt 3 accessories and hubs can cause trouble for nearby wireless devices, too.

The simplest fix Apple lists is also the most obvious one: "Bring your Wi-Fi device closer to your Wi-Fi router." Its guide also says most routers look for the least crowded channel when they start up or reset.

## What Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 add

Wi-Fi comes in numbered generations. The two newest, Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7, can use the 6 GHz band. Apple's [Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102285) says that band can give "faster and more reliable Wi-Fi connections."

The catch is that both ends need to support it. You need a Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 router, and a device that can use it. On Apple's list, that includes the iPhone 15 Pro and later (but not the base iPhone 15, 15 Plus, 16e or 17e), Macs from 2023 onward, and the [Apple HomeView](../apple-homeview/) smart display. Apple also notes 6 GHz features aren't available in every region. Older devices still connect to the same router on 2.4 or 5 GHz.

## What it means for you

- **Slow in one room, fast in another?** That's usually distance and walls, not your internet plan.
- **The kitchen can be a weak spot.** Microwaves, metal appliances and pipes full of water are all on Apple's list.
- **A new router alone doesn't unlock 6 GHz.** Your phone or laptop has to support it too.
- **Wi-Fi isn't the only thing that fails.** Sometimes the app itself is down, which we cover in [why apps go down](../why-do-apps-go-down/).

## Is it worth caring about?

Our take: knowing the basics matters most to people with bigger homes, thick walls or a router tucked in a cabinet, and to anyone who has ever paid for a faster plan and seen no difference. For everyone else, it's handy to know that "the internet is slow" and "the Wi-Fi is weak" are two different problems.
