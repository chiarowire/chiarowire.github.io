The Microsoft 365 outage is over. Starting on Thursday, November 12, 2026, Outlook, Teams, Copilot and other Microsoft 365 apps stopped working for huge numbers of people around the world. Microsoft says it found the problem and restored service to the last affected customers on Saturday, November 14. It says there was no sign of a cyberattack.

## What happened in the Microsoft 365 outage

Problems started shortly before 1 p.m. Eastern time on Thursday, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/microsoft-outage-outlook-aws-google.html). [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/12/microsofts-outlook-email-hit-by-new-outage/) reports that it was first flagged in the US and the Americas, then spread worldwide while engineers worked to bring servers back online.

Here's what went down, based on both reports:

| Service | What people use it for |
|---|---|
| Outlook | Email and calendars, for work and personal accounts |
| Teams | Video calls and work chat |
| SharePoint and online files | Shared documents |
| Copilot | Microsoft's AI assistant |
| Admin and security tools | IT teams managing company computers |

The damage reached past office workers. CNBC reports that some UK National Health Service services were affected, that Air India temporarily rerouted its email, and that at least one company pushed back its earnings report.

Recovery was slow. By Friday morning on the US East Coast, Outlook had started to come back and some delayed emails were arriving, TechCrunch reported. Microsoft said Saturday that it had "addressed the underlying issue" and restored the remaining customers and regions (CNBC).

## What caused it?

Microsoft's explanation, as reported by CNBC: the disruption came from "an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade." In plainer words, work to add more room to Microsoft's network went wrong.

Early on, Microsoft described the symptom as "a large number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable" (TechCrunch). When some servers drop out, the remaining ones have to carry their traffic, and that extra load can knock more of them over. Microsoft also said some servers failed automatic health checks, which are the routine tests a system runs to decide whether a server is fit to take traffic.

Microsoft told TechCrunch there was "no evidence of malicious activity" and that the incident was not related to AI or AI agents. CNBC reports that Microsoft will publish a full report once it finishes its review. Until then, that's the whole official explanation. Anything beyond it hasn't been announced.

## What it means for you

If you use Outlook or Teams, especially through work or school, you may have lost a day or more of email and meetings. A few things are worth knowing:

- **Delayed email.** Microsoft extended the time it keeps trying to deliver held messages to 48 hours, to protect mail stuck in the queue, CNBC reports. So emails sent during the outage were held for longer rather than dropped right away.
- **Microsoft apologized.** A spokesperson told CNBC: "We are deeply sorry for the disruption this incident has caused our customers and partners."
- **Down isn't the same as deleted.** Neither report mentions any lost data. An outage like this generally means the servers that hand you your email couldn't be reached, not that the email itself was gone. ## Is it worth caring about?

Our take: yes, even if you don't use Microsoft apps, because this kind of outage shows how much of daily life runs through a handful of companies' data centers.

"The cloud" is really other people's computers, rented out. That's usually great: it's cheap, it's everywhere, and it rarely breaks. But when one big provider has a bad day, millions of people feel it at once. TechCrunch notes this is not Microsoft's first long cloud outage this year: a multi-day one in September 2026 was blamed on a "core authentication configuration" issue.

It also wasn't the only bad day this week. CNBC reports that Amazon Web Services and Google users also reported problems on Thursday, though Amazon said its services were operating normally. The internet is built to route around trouble (our guide to [how the internet works](../how-does-the-internet-work/) explains how traffic finds another road), but a single company's apps can still stop when that company's own systems fail.

Who this matters to most: people who rely on Outlook and Teams for work or school, and the IT teams who keep those systems running.
