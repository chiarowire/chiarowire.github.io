The Chase bank outage on Monday, November 16, 2026, came from what JPMorgan Chase called a software bug in its own hardware systems, not a cyberattack. Some transactions took longer than usual, and Zelle payments between Chase and other banks were delayed. Chase said customers could still access their accounts and funds.

## What happened in the Chase bank outage

People started reporting errors as early as 11:30 a.m. Eastern on Monday, [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/16/jpmorgan-chase-technical-issue.html) reports. [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2318431/customers-at-major-banks-report-difficulty-accessing-accounts-online/) says reports for some banks began climbing around 10 a.m., with others spiking in the afternoon.

By 3 p.m. Eastern, the outage tracker Downdetector listed Chase, Bank of America, U.S. Bank and Wells Fargo as the top four services with reported problems, according to Engadget. Zelle, Capital One, PNC and Truist also saw spikes. Downdetector counts reports from users, so a spike shows that people noticed trouble, not what caused it.

Here's the day in one table, based on CNBC and Engadget:

## What caused it?

Chase described it as a "temporary issue related to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems," per CNBC. "On-premise" just means computers the bank runs itself, in its own buildings, rather than servers rented from a cloud company. (Our explainer on [where cloud files actually live](../where-are-cloud-photos-stored/) covers the difference.)

A Chase spokesperson told CNBC, "There is no evidence it is cyber-related." Chase also told the Associated Press that the problem had nothing to do with cybersecurity, Engadget reports.

Why did other banks show up in the reports? Chase said other banks "may have been impacted by slowness in transactions," per CNBC. When money moves between two banks, both sides have to process it, so a slowdown at one big bank can hold up transfers at others. U.S. Bank said it was "not directly impacted," but that clients sending or receiving Zelle payments with another institution might see delays (CNBC).

## What it means for you

- **Slower, not missing.** The banks' statements describe payments taking longer than normal. Neither report we checked mentions lost money or lost data.
- **Zelle wasn't down everywhere.** The delays mainly involved payments where a bank on one side of the transfer was affected, based on the statements CNBC and Engadget quote.
- **A tracker spike isn't proof of a problem at every bank listed.** Downdetector reflects what users report. When one big bank has trouble, people often check their other apps too.
- **Fix timing.** On Monday afternoon, Chase said it was "working diligently to resolve this by the end of the day today," per CNBC.

## Is it worth caring about?

Our take: yes, mostly as a reminder of how connected everyday money apps are.

Paying a friend or getting your paycheck feels like one tap. Behind it, at least two banks' systems, plus services like Zelle that link them, all have to work at the same moment. That design is usually fast and reliable. But when one large piece slows down, people at several banks can feel it. Our guide to [how the internet works](../how-does-the-internet-work/) explains why networks can route around trouble, while a single company's own systems can still stall.

It was also a rough week for big tech systems. Engadget points out that several Microsoft services went down on November 12 (we covered the [Microsoft 365 outage](../microsoft-365-outage/)), and that air traffic control equipment problems led to a nationwide ground stop the next day. The companies involved have described these as separate problems.

Who this matters to most: Chase customers, and anyone who sent or expected a Zelle payment on Monday. For everyone else, it's a look at why your banking app depends on far more than your phone. Money apps are also where AI is heading next, as OpenAI's [ChatGPT Wallet](../chatgpt-wallet/) shows, which makes reliable payment systems matter even more.
